Do I Need to Patent My Logo?

Published 2026-11-16

TL;DR: No, you usually do not patent a logo. Logos are typically protected, if at all, by trademark, and sometimes by copyright. The first place to check is the USPTO trademark database, then other official records if ownership is not obvious.

Short answer: a logo is usually not patented, it is typically protected, if at all, by trademark and sometimes copyright.

A patent protects inventions, not logos. A logo is usually a brand identifier, so the first question is whether it is being used as a source identifier. The practical next step is to check the USPTO trademark database. If the logo is original artwork, copyright can also matter, but that is a separate issue.

What legal protection a logo can have instead of a patent

Trademark protection is about brand use. If a logo tells people, “this product or service comes from that business,” trademark law is the part you care about.

Copyright protection is about original creative expression. If the logo is a distinctive drawing, illustration, or design, copyright may protect that artwork as art, even if the logo also functions as a trademark.

So the same logo can wear two hats. One says, “this is the source identifier.” The other says, “this is original creative work.”

How to check whether the logo is already protected in official records

Start with a plain-word search in the USPTO trademark database. Search the brand name, any logo wording, and close variations. If the logo is registered or applied for as a trademark, the USPTO record should usually show the owner, filing history, and basic status details. That is why Are trademarks public records? is a useful companion read, because it explains why the USPTO record is the cleanest place to start.

If the brand owner is not obvious from the USPTO record, or the business name differs from the trademark owner, cross-check other official sources. EUIPO can help for European Union trademarks, WIPO can help for international filings, SEC EDGAR can help when a public company is involved, and state business registries can help when the legal entity behind the name is organized locally. The point is to find the legal owner in official records, not to rely on the brand display name.

When a logo might involve more than one owner or record

Do not assume the brand name equals ownership. The trademark owner, business name, parent company, and filing entity can all be different. Sometimes the entity that uses the logo is not the same entity that owns the trademark. Sometimes a parent company files through a subsidiary. Sometimes the business name on the storefront and the legal owner in the filing are different.

That is why it helps to verify the legal owner in the official record rather than guessing from the brand display name. If the ownership trail is unclear, How to use SEC filings to find who owns a brand can help because SEC filings may show the control chain when a trademark record does not tell the whole story.

Useful example: a small business logo search

Imagine you see a coffee shop logo on cups, signs, and Instagram posts. First, search the logo text, or the shop name if the logo has wording, in the USPTO database.

If you find a trademark record, open it and check the listed owner. That owner might be the shop itself, a separate company, or a parent entity. If the record shows a legal entity that matches the store operator, that is a good sign you have found the right owner.

If the owner name and the day-to-day business name do not match, stop treating the brand name as the legal answer. Check the state business registry for the operating company, and if the brand belongs to a public company, use SEC records to see whether a parent or affiliate is the filing entity. That is the part where ownership stops being simple and starts being paperwork.

What to do if you want stronger protection for your logo

First, check the logo in the USPTO trademark database. Second, decide whether trademark filing fits the way the logo is used, meaning whether it works as a brand identifier. Third, if the logo is original artwork, remember copyright may also be relevant for the design itself.

If ownership is unclear, or if the logo sits inside a larger brand structure with parents, subsidiaries, or separate filing entities, get legal review before you file or rely on the mark. That is the safest way to avoid building your brand on the wrong name.
