Difference Between Trademark and Copyright

Published 2026-10-31

TL;DR: Trademark and copyright are different rights: trademark protects brand identifiers like names and logos, while copyright protects original creative works like text, art, photos, music, and software. If you are trying to find who owns a brand, trademark records such as the USPTO database are usually the right starting point, while copyright records usually are not.

Opening: trademark vs. copyright in plain English

Trademark protects source identifiers for brands, like names and logos. Copyright protects original creative expression, like writing, artwork, photos, music, and software. That distinction matters because if you are trying to figure out who owns a brand, you need to know whether you are looking at a brand marker or a creative work. Different right, different record, different rabbit hole.

What a trademark covers

A trademark is basically a brand identifier. Think names, logos, slogans, and other signals that tell people where goods or services come from. In official trademark records, ownership usually ties back to the legal registrant listed in the registry data, such as the USPTO database for U.S. marks. When you are checking a brand, that registrant field is the part that usually does the heavy lifting.

What copyright covers

Copyright protects original creative works. That can include written copy, artwork, photos, music, and software. It is a different legal right from trademark protection, even when the same business created both. So a logo file, a product photo, and a website banner can all be different kinds of assets.

Key differences readers should use when checking brand ownership

Here is the practical side-by-side version:

Topic Trademark Copyright
Purpose Identifies the source of a brand, product, or service Protects original creative expression
What it protects Names, logos, slogans, and similar source indicators Text, artwork, photos, music, software, and other original works
Where to look for records Trademark registries, such as the USPTO database for U.S. marks Copyright records or other copyright office databases, depending on the country
What the record can prove Who the registrant or recorded owner is in that trademark system Who filed or registered the copyright record, if a record exists
What it cannot prove It does not automatically explain every contract, license, or outside ownership issue It does not answer who owns the brand name or logo as a source identifier

The short version: trademark records are about brand identity. Copyright records are about creative works. Same company can show up in both places, but one registry does not replace the other.

Where trademark records fit into an ownership search

If someone is trying to find who owns a brand, trademark records are usually the better starting point. That is because official trademark registry data can identify the trademark owner or registrant. Copyright records usually do not answer brand ownership questions, because they are built to track creative works, not source identifiers.

Worked example: a brand name, logo, and website image

Say you are looking at a coffee brand called North Harbor Roasters.

The brand name and logo are the first things to check in trademark records, because those are classic source identifiers. If the USPTO database lists a registrant for the word mark or logo mark, that record is your starting point for brand ownership.

Now look at the homepage. The hero photo of a latte, the menu copy, and the promotional banner art are different assets. Those are more likely to be copyright territory, because they are creative works. So if you are checking who owns the brand name, go to trademark records. If you are checking who owns the photo or graphic, that is a copyright question.

How to use this distinction in the USPTO search flow

This is the part where people sometimes poke the wrong registry and wonder why the answer is acting shy. The USPTO database is for trademark searches, so it helps you look up trademark owners, registrants, and related mark data. It is not a copyright registry.

So the rule of thumb is simple, use the right database for the right right. Trademark questions start in trademark records, especially the USPTO for U.S. marks. Copyright questions start in copyright records, not in the trademark search flow.

Optional closing FAQ

Can one asset be protected by both trademark and copyright?

Yes. A logo, for example, can function as a trademark if it identifies a brand source, and it can also be copyrighted if it contains original creative expression. The two rights can overlap, but they do different jobs, so you still need to check the right registry for the question you are asking.
