What Is a DBA and How Do You Look One Up?

Published 2026-10-30

TL;DR: A DBA, short for “doing business as,” is a public business name that can differ from the legal name in official records. To look one up, search the official state or county filing office where the name was registered, then check the record for the legal entity, status, and filing details.

A DBA is a business name that differs from the legal name on the paperwork. It stands for “doing business as,” and it is also commonly called a trade name, fictitious name, or assumed name, depending on the jurisdiction. This trade name differs from the business name that appears on official records, but it’s still valid for you to use, as long as it’s properly registered. As one DBA guide explains, a DBA links the legal name of an entity to the name under which it conducts business, while the legal name still appears in official records (source).

What a DBA means in business records

In business records, a DBA is the public-facing name a business uses when it does not want to operate only under its legal name. The legal entity is still the legal entity. The DBA is the extra label on top, like a storefront sign on a building with a different name in the deed file.

Different states may call this filing a trade name, fictitious name, assumed name, or something similar. The point is the same: the name people see in the market is not always the name in the registry.

Why someone would look up a DBA

People look up DBAs to figure out who is behind a brand name. That usually means checking whether the name is registered, then finding the legal business name or owner information tied to it.

A DBA search can help when you know the brand, but not the company. Handy, because businesses love names that sound like one thing while the filing record says another.

Where to look up a DBA

Start with the official office where the name was registered. That is usually a state business registry, a county clerk, or another local filing office, depending on the jurisdiction.

If you are looking at trademarks, use the USPTO search tools for that purpose. A USPTO search helps with trademarks, not DBA filings, unless the business name is also being used as a trademark. Different record, different animal.

How to look up a DBA in plain words

  1. Figure out the most likely jurisdiction. If the business operates locally, that might be a county office. If the state handles the filing, start there.
  2. Search the state or county business records. Use the official registry or clerk site, not a random directory.
  3. Try name variations. Search the exact brand name, shortened versions, punctuation changes, and common spelling tweaks.
  4. Open the filing record. Look for the legal business name, registration status, and the filing office details.

If the record is there, it usually tells you enough to connect the public name to the registered entity.

Worked example: tracing a brand name back to the filing record

Say you search a brand-style name like “Blue Ridge Coffee Co.” in a state registry. The result may not list a company called exactly that. Instead, it might show a filing for a legal entity such as “Blue Ridge Hospitality LLC” with “Blue Ridge Coffee Co.” recorded as the assumed or fictitious name.

That is the useful bit. The storefront name is what customers see. The filing record is what tells you which legal entity is behind it.

How a DBA lookup differs from a trademark lookup

A DBA lookup asks, “What business filed this name?” A trademark lookup asks, “Is this name protected as a brand identifier in trademark records?”

The USPTO database is the right place for trademark research. It is not a substitute for a DBA search, because a DBA filing does not automatically mean the name is a trademark, and a trademark record does not automatically tell you where a local DBA was filed.

So if you are checking who is behind a brand, a DBA search helps with the filing trail. If you are checking brand rights, the USPTO matters. Same name, different paperwork drama.

When a DBA search is not enough

A DBA record can point you to the legal name behind a brand, but it may not prove brand ownership by itself.

For fuller ownership research, you may also need to check:

  • state entity records, to confirm the legal business entity
  • USPTO records, for trademark information
  • SEC or other official registries, depending on the business and the kind of information you need

In other words, a DBA lookup is a strong clue. It is not always the whole case file.

FAQ

Does a DBA mean the same thing in every state? No. The concept is broadly similar, but the label and filing rules can vary by state or local jurisdiction.
