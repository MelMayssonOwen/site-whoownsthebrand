How to Check Shareholders of a Company

Published 2026-11-06

TL;DR: To check shareholders of a company, start with the legal entity behind the brand, then use SEC EDGAR, state business registries, and trademark records to trace the owner and any public ownership disclosures. Public companies may reveal shareholder or control information in filings, while private companies often do not make shareholder information public.

Start with the legal name, not just the brand name

If you want to find a company’s shareholders, begin with the legal entity that sits behind the brand. Shareholders may be disclosed in the registrant's filings, not always in the polished name on the storefront or website. So identify the exact legal name first, then follow ownership from that point. Otherwise you are essentially asking the wrong filing office to solve the wrong mystery.

Check the company’s official filings in the SEC EDGAR database

If the company files with the SEC, EDGAR is the first place to look. Search EDGAR for the company name, then open the actual filing, not just the search result. The filing can show the legal entity, parent company, and ownership disclosures that point to major shareholders or control relationships. That is the useful part. The search hit alone is just a breadcrumb.

Look in state business registry records

State corporate registries can help you connect a brand to the legal entity behind it. They can confirm the registered legal name, entity status, and sometimes officer or organizer details. That does not automatically give you a shareholder list, but it does help you map the brand name to the company that owns or operates it.

This step is especially useful when the brand name and legal name do not match, which happens a lot. Companies love a public-facing alias.

Use trademark records only to connect the brand to an owner, not to list shareholders

USPTO, EUIPO, and WIPO records can help you identify who owns a trademarked brand. That is useful. But those records usually do not show shareholders. They are a starting point for the company-owner search, not the finish line.

Worked example: tracing a brand name to the company that likely holds the shares

Say you start with a brand name you found on packaging or a website.

  1. Search the brand in trademark records, such as USPTO, EUIPO, or WIPO.
  2. Note the trademark owner or registrant name.
  3. Search that name in SEC EDGAR if the owner appears to be a public company or SEC filer.
  4. If there is no SEC filing, check the relevant state business registry for the legal entity.
  5. Open the filing or registry record and confirm the exact legal name, then look for ownership disclosures, parent-subsidiary relationships, or control information.

At the end of that trail, you may find the shareholder information you need, or you may find that the public record stops at the registrant or owner. That is still a useful answer, even if it is not the glamorous one.

What to do when the company is private

Private companies may not have SEC filings, so EDGAR may not give you anything useful. In that case, rely on state registry records, trademark ownership, and any regulator-specific filings that are available. Just keep your expectations realistic. Shareholder information for private companies is often not public, and the paperwork does not always volunteer itself for interrogation.

What to verify before relying on a result

Before you treat any result as solid, check these basics:

  • Exact legal name, not just the brand name
  • Entity type, such as corporation or LLC
  • Current status in the registry
  • Filing date, so you know how current the record is
  • Whether the record shows a parent, subsidiary, or registrant instead of an actual shareholder list

That last one matters a lot. A registrant is not the same thing as a full shareholder roster, and the paperwork will not politely correct you.
