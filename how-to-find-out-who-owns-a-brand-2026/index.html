How to find out who really owns a brand (2026)

To find out who owns a brand, start with its trademark: search the name in the USPTO Trademark Search, open the record in TSDR, and read the current registered owner. If that owner is a subsidiary, trace transfers in the Assignment Center and find the ultimate parent in the company’s SEC EDGAR filings. The whole method is free and takes about five minutes.

The five-minute method, step by step

  1. Find the trademark owner of record. Search the brand name in USPTO Trademark Search. Open the matching registration in TSDR and read the Owner field. This is the legal entity that owns the U.S. mark right now.
  2. Trace how ownership moved. Brands change hands. The USPTO Assignment Center shows recorded assignments, who transferred the mark to whom, and on what date. This is how you catch acquisitions the marketing never mentions.
  3. Resolve the ultimate parent. If the owner is a subsidiary or holding company, find who owns it. For public companies, SEC EDGAR full-text search and the annual report (10-K or 20-F) list owned brands and subsidiaries. For private entities, use OpenCorporates.
  4. Check international marks. The WIPO Global Brand Database covers marks filed outside the U.S. and through the Madrid System, useful when a brand is foreign-owned.
  5. Confirm the operating entity. OpenCorporates confirms the company’s jurisdiction, status, and (where available) corporate group, closing the chain.

Announced deal vs recorded transfer, the mistake to avoid

The single biggest error in brand-ownership research is treating a press announcement as the moment ownership changed. It usually is not. An acquisition is typically announced, then closed weeks or months later, and the trademark assignment is recorded later still. Databases then take more time to reflect it. When precision matters, cite the recorded assignment date from the USPTO, not the news date, and note both.

A live example: Unilever bought Ben & Jerry’s in 2000, but in 2025 the brand’s ultimate parent changed again when Unilever demerged its ice cream business into The Magnum Ice Cream Company. A source that stopped at “Unilever” is now out of date. Ownership is a moving target, always check the record’s date.

Why the trademark owner is often not a name you recognise

Conglomerates hold brands through subsidiaries and holding companies for liability, tax, and administrative reasons. That is why the mark for a “natural” or “indie” brand may be registered to an unfamiliar LLC, which is itself owned by a large public parent. The trademark assignment record is what connects the two. Frequently asked questions

What is the fastest way to find out who owns a brand?

Search the brand name in USPTO Trademark Search, open the live record in TSDR, and read the 'Owner' field. That gives you the registered trademark owner in under a minute. If that owner is a subsidiary, one more step (the parent's SEC filing) gives you the ultimate parent.

Is who owns a brand public record?

Yes. U.S. trademark registrations, their owners, and recorded ownership transfers (assignments) are public at the USPTO. Public companies also disclose the brands they own in annual SEC filings. You can confirm ownership for free.

Why does the trademark show an unfamiliar LLC as the owner?

Large companies hold brands through subsidiary or holding entities for tax, liability, and administrative reasons. The LLC on the trademark is the legal owner, but it may itself be owned by a parent. Follow the assignment record and corporate filings up the chain to the ultimate parent.

How current is trademark ownership data?

USPTO records reflect what has actually been recorded. There is often a lag between a deal being announced in the press and the trademark assignment being recorded, and between a recorded assignment and databases updating. Always distinguish an announced acquisition from a recorded transfer, and check the record date.

Do I need a paid tool to check brand ownership?

No. The USPTO, SEC EDGAR, WIPO, and OpenCorporates are all free and authoritative. Paid tools mainly save time by assembling the chain for you; they are a convenience, not a requirement.
